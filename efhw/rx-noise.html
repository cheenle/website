Diagnose before you buy · the source-by-source verdict on chokes

Where received noise
actually gets in

Every dB of received noise enters through one of four doors. They have different causes, different signatures and different cures, and a common-mode choke only reaches one of them. This page is about finding out which door your noise uses before you spend money, because on a quiet station the honest answer is that you should buy nothing at all. Every claim carries a C for a checkable source or an 算式 for arithmetic you can redo on paper.

01 · Measure first, buy later

Four doors, and the cuts that separate them

Received noise enters an EFHW station through four paths. The ARRL’s own guidance for interference hunting is to ask “where is this coming from” before asking “what is producing it”, and the same discipline pays here: you cannot pick the right cure until you know the path.

Door Path Coupling How it shows up
① Element The antenna wire picks the noise up directly. Near-field electric, and far field Cut 2 barely changes it
② Shield Common-mode current on the outside of the coax braid, running through the noise field all the way from the feedpoint to the shack. Conducted along the braid, plus field coupling Cut 2 collapses it; a clip-on bead changes it
③ Mains Noise conducted in on the supply wiring, through the PSU, into the receiver’s ground reference. Conducted Cut 4 changes it
④ Near field The rig, the SDR, a USB lead or an Ethernet run is itself the receiving antenna. Near-field Cut 5 makes the noise follow the device
Read the last column as a pointer, not a proof. Each cut narrows the candidates; none of them identifies a culprit on its own. You are eliminating doors, one at a time, until one is left standing.

Five cuts, in the order they pay

All five fit in one afternoon and need nothing you do not already own. Cut 1 carries the most information per minute, so start there and stop as soon as a door is eliminated.

Cut What you do What the reading means
1 Screw a 50 Ω dummy load onto the antenna port and listen on the same frequency. Noise disappears → it arrives via the antenna system (door ① or ②). Noise stays → it comes in on the mains or from the near field (door ③ or ④). This one cut halves the search space.
2 Disconnect the antenna wire at the transformer but leave the coax connected and running to the shack. Noise stays → the braid is receiving it, and a choke has a target (door ②). Noise collapses → the antenna wire itself is doing the listening, and no choke will help (door ①).
3 Clip a ferrite bead onto the coax and A/B it in and out, several times. Level steps with the bead → you have confirmed a common-mode path, and a permanent choke is worth building. Level moves but not back, or moves only at some frequencies → you are shifting a resonance, not removing a current (see the divider section).
4 Run the radio from a battery and unplug every charger, every wall-wart and every Ethernet run in the room. Noise changes → door ③, and the cure belongs on the mains lead, not on the coax.
5 Walk the house with a portable shortwave receiver, holding it close to suspect devices. Noise rises sharply near one device → door ④, and you have found the culprit. Noise that is uniform everywhere points back at doors ①, ② or ③.
Two rules, or nothing below this line is trustworthy.
1. Relative readings only. An S-meter reading is not a noise power measurement. It moves with AGC, with preamp and attenuator settings, and — the part people miss — with the source impedance the receiver is looking at. Swapping in a dummy load changes that impedance, so part of the level shift in cut 1 is not a noise change at all. Compare, never measure absolutely.
2. Same hour, same frequency, same settings. The ambient noise field moves over minutes and hours as appliances and neighbours come and go. An A/B split across two evenings proves nothing.

02 · Six sources, four doors

The same phrase covers six different things

“Environmental interference” gets used as one problem. It is six. Read the cards as a comparison rather than as six essays: every card uses the same five headings, so the verdict on chokes can be read straight down the column and the differences are the point.

1 · Power lines C

Mechanism. Almost all power-line noise comes from a spark or an arc across utility hardware: a gap between two conductors, caused by fittings that are loose, broken or spaced too closely. Current crosses the gap, ionises the air, and radiates broadband energy.

Signature. On a 50 Hz grid the arcing fires 100 times a second, because every cycle has two voltage peaks and two zero crossings. On a 60 Hz grid it is 120 Hz. On an oscilloscope the noise appears as two horizontal bands drifting slowly upward, one from each half of the cycle. Two free tests confirm it: rain quietens it, because water bridges the gap, and wind makes it vary, because loose hardware moves.

A correction worth having. Corona discharge is rarely, if ever, the cause. The ARRL states it plainly: corona is confined to lower frequencies, it is a low-current phenomenon, and it does not propagate far from the source. Blaming corona sends you looking for the wrong thing on the wrong pole.

CMC verdict: no. The energy travels along the power wiring and reaches you by radiation into the antenna wire — door ①. A choke acts on the coax braid’s common-mode path, and this noise is not on it.

Remedy. Complain, with evidence. This is the one category where the fix belongs to somebody else, and in many jurisdictions that somebody is legally obliged to act.

Source: ARRL, Power Line Noise · IEEE Std 1897-2024

2 · Switching supplies C 算式

Mechanism. A switching converter chops DC at tens to hundreds of kilohertz. The harmonics of that chop, and the ringing on every edge, run straight up through the HF spectrum. The noise leaves the box in two forms: differential mode between live and neutral, which an X capacitor suppresses, and common mode between the conductors and earth, which needs a Y capacitor and a common-mode choke. Cheap supplies cut cost by omitting precisely those last two parts — the only two that work at HF.

Signature. A comb of harmonics spaced at the switching frequency, drifting as the supply’s load changes. Often accompanied by a slow periodic wobble as the supply enters and leaves burst mode on light load.

What compliance actually buys you. Household equipment must meet the Class B conducted-emission limits. Here they are, with the voltage limit converted to the power it represents across 50 Ω:

CMC verdict: no, for the coax. Noise entering on the mains never touches the coax, so a choke there is decoration.

Remedy. The same device, on the right conductor: a common-mode choke on the mains lead or the DC lead. Also check USB and Ethernet runs, which are the most commonly missed members of this family.

Source: GB/T 9254.1-2021 (≈ CISPR 32) · EMC practice for X and Y capacitors and common-mode chokes

3 · Electric vehicle chargers C

Mechanism. AC charging runs the mains through an on-board charger in the car — a switching converter of 3.3 to 11 kW. DC fast charging does the conversion in the post itself, at even higher power, increasingly with silicon-carbide devices and their steeper switching edges. Either way the charging cable is several metres of multi-conductor cable running from a building to a vehicle, and its protective earth conductor is exactly as long as the common-mode antenna it forms. A kilowatt-class switching converter driving a long unshielded conductor, with no RF reference ground at either end, is the mechanism written out in full.

Signature. Broadband, strongest while a car is actually charging, and gone when it unplugs. That on/off correlation is the most useful thing about it, because it dates and fingerprints the event even when you cannot see the charger.

No public HF measurement exists for this. Every reachable published case about EV chargers concerns conducted-emission test failures, or the 125 kHz band where charging posts interfere with keyless-entry systems. Nothing we could retrieve measures a charger at 1.8–30 MHz. What you have just read is mechanism only. We will not print a number we cannot show, and neither should anyone else selling you a solution.

CMC verdict: partly. The dominant path is door ① — the charging cable radiates into your antenna wire. A coax choke reaches only the fraction that arrives on the shield.

Remedy. Negotiation and geometry, in that order. Establish the on/off correlation first; that is the evidence that makes a conversation with the owner or the property manager possible. Then move your own antenna further away, or accept the hours.

Source: GB/T 18487.1-2015 · published EV-charger EMC remediation literature · 125 kHz keyless-entry case reports

4 · Solar inverters C

Mechanism. Modern string inverters are usually transformerless, so the panels and the grid share a common-mode path with no isolation between them. Add the long DC run down from the roof, which forms a large common-mode loop, and switching devices with steep edges, and you have a strong common-mode source bolted to the same building as your antenna.

Signature. Correlated with daylight and with inverter output. Often strongest at midday, absent at night, and it changes when a cloud crosses.

CMC verdict: partly. Mixed paths. The fraction radiated into the antenna is out of reach; the fraction conducted into the building wiring is reachable, but with a choke on the mains, not on the coax.

Remedy. Almost entirely source-side, which means not yours: LCL filters, common-mode reactors, active filtering, and gate-drive optimisation all live in the inverter. What you own is the same on/off evidence, and the same conversation.

Source: ARRL RFI programme, which lists solar alongside power lines and lighting as its three tracked categories

5 · Power-line communication (PLC / BPL) C

Mechanism. Broadband over Power Line is defined as carrying data over power wiring in the 2–30 MHz band. That is not adjacent to the amateur HF bands. It is the amateur HF bands, plus everything between them.

Signature. This is the one entry in this list that is not a by-product. It is a modulated, deliberate signal inside your band, and it sounds like one.

CMC verdict: no, and a choke will never help. The signal arrives in band, on the antenna, as the wanted signal would. There is no common-mode path to interrupt.

Remedy. Frequency coordination and complaint. This is a regulatory problem, and treating it as an antenna problem wastes money.

Source: BPL band definition (2–30 MHz) · national RFI complaint procedure

6 · LED lighting and grow lights C

Mechanism. Every LED lamp has a driver, and the driver is a small switching supply built to the tightest possible budget. The same cost pressure described in card 2 applies, at a smaller scale and with less room for filtering. High-power ballasts for grow lights are the industrial version of the same story, and the ARRL names them as a category it fields a steady stream of questions about.

Signature. Tied to a switch. The noise appears when a lamp is on and vanishes when it is off, which makes cut 5 the decisive test and makes this the easiest source on the list to identify.

CMC verdict: partly. Whether a choke helps depends entirely on which door the noise used to get in, which is why the diagnostic comes first.

Remedy. Replace the lamp with a better-behaved one. Cheaper and more reliable than filtering the consequences.

Source: ARRL RFI programme (lighting devices category)

Eight rows, and only the last one is where a coax choke does its full job. This is the whole page in one object.

One rule generates the whole table. A choke acts only on the common-mode path in the conductor you put it on. Three consequences follow, and they explain every “no” above:
1. It does nothing about noise the antenna wire picks up directly, because that noise is not on the coax.
2. It does nothing about noise conducted in on the mains, because that noise never reaches the coax either.
3. It does nothing once mode conversion has already happened, because by then the noise has left the path the choke can reach.
03 · Door ②: the feedline shield

A choke is a mode selector, not a filter

This is the section that most choke articles skip, and it is the one that decides whether the device can work at all. Get the mechanism right and the placements, the failures and the recipes all follow from it.

The sentence that explains everything

A receiver responds to one thing only: the differential voltage between the inner conductor and the inside surface of the braid. Common-mode current flowing on the outside of the braid cannot reach the receiver by itself. It first has to be converted into a differential voltage, and that conversion happens at an asymmetry — the imperfect balance of the 49:1 transformer, the feedpoint, the rig’s chassis ground. The braid is a receiving antenna that is wired straight into a mode converter you cannot remove.

An ideal choke presents almost zero impedance to differential mode and a high impedance to common mode. Your wanted signal travels in differential mode and passes through untouched. The noise riding on the braid is blocked. That is the whole mechanism. It also explains what a choke does not do: it does not improve your match, raise efficiency or change SWR, because none of those have anything to do with the common-mode path. A choke that changed your SWR was changing something else, and you should find out what.

How much it actually removes

Inserting a series impedance into the common-mode loop divides the current down. To first order:

A ≈ 20 · log₁₀( 1 + Z_cm / Z_loop )

Z_loop is not 50 Ω. It is the impedance of the noise source’s own common-mode loop — the house wiring, the neighbour’s appliance, the building’s ground path. It varies wildly with frequency and you usually do not know it. Read the left column as a shopping warning. A clip-on bead has a Z_cm in the tens of ohms across most of HF. Look at the top row: that buys you a handful of dB at best. To do anything serious the choke needs a Z_cm in the kilohm range, and that means a proper core with a proper number of turns.

Three limits of that model

The divider is a first-order picture and it is wrong in three directions that matter in the field.

1. Z_cm is complex. It is R + jX, and the resistive part is the useful part. A choke that is purely reactive absorbs nothing; it stores energy and returns it somewhere else, which is how you get resonances rather than attenuation.

2. Inserting Z_cm changes the current distribution. The model pretends the loop is fixed and you are just adding a resistor. In reality the coax is electrically long, current maxima move, and a single choke often relocates a resonance instead of removing a current. The classic symptom is “it is better on 7 MHz and now worse on 5 MHz”. If you see that, you moved the problem, you did not solve it.

3. Which is why there are two chokes, not two options. One at the feedpoint, one where the coax enters the shack. Between them the line is broken into sections, and if each section is shorter than a quarter wave none of them can resonate. The two are not alternatives; they do different jobs.

Z_cm is a function of frequency, and it is not flat

Turns give you impedance as Z_cm ∝ N², but the material’s permeability is itself a function of frequency. Measured values from the Fair-Rite material data, for the two mixes you will actually use:

For a choke it is good news, because a lossy choke absorbs the energy instead of storing and returning it, which is exactly the behaviour you want at point 1 above. Different job, different verdict on the same material.

The asymmetry that receive work hands you

Received levels are microvolts. A ferrite on a receive path never saturates, so the entire power-handling and heating discipline that governs transmitter-side chokes simply does not apply.

That cuts both ways. If a choke serves only receive — a separate receiving antenna, a T/R-split front end — you can pile on turns and use a high-permeability mix, and reach a Z_cm far above anything a transmit path could survive. If the choke sits in the main path it must carry transmit current too, and it is bound by saturation and heat.

The best choke for receive may be one that would burn on transmit. Keep that in mind before copying somebody’s recipe: ask which side of the T/R switch their numbers came from.

Counterpoise is a premise, not an option

First the counterpoise, then the choke. An end-fed antenna needs a return path. With no return path, adding a choke just turns the choke into part of the antenna’s load, and the common-mode current finds a third route out — through the chassis, down the mains, into everything you were trying to protect. A choke does not replace a counterpoise; a counterpoise is what lets a choke do its job.
The feedpoint trap. If the coax itself is the counterpoise, then choking at the feedpoint cuts the antenna’s own return path: SWR shifts and efficiency drops. In that arrangement the choke has to sit away from the feedpoint — the ARRL Handbook figure is at least 10 feet, about 3 m. If instead you have a separate counterpoise wire, the feedpoint is the best place to choke, because the current you are intercepting is exactly the current the antenna wanted to drive onto the braid.
