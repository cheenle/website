Find the path first. Then buy something — or nothing.

Where receive noise
actually gets in

Noise reaches your receiver by four different paths. They come from different places and need different fixes, and a common-mode choke reaches only one of them. So measure first — on a quiet station the right answer is to buy nothing at all. Every number below is marked C when you can look up the source, or 算式 when you can redo the arithmetic yourself.

01 · Check first, buy later

Four paths, and five cuts that tell them apart

You cannot pick the right fix until you know how the noise gets in. The ARRL gives its own interference hunters the same instruction: work out where it is coming from before worrying about what is making it.

Path What it is How it couples What it looks like
① Antenna wire The antenna picks the noise up the same way it picks up signals. Near-field electric field, and far field Cut 2 leaves it almost unchanged
② Coax shield Current on the outside of the braid, flowing along the whole length of coax between feedpoint and shack. Along the braid, and from nearby fields Cut 2 kills it; a clip-on bead changes it
③ Mains wiring Noise arrives on the power line, goes through the supply, and ends up on the chassis the receiver measures against. Conducted Cut 4 changes it
④ Near field The rig, the SDR, a USB lead or a network cable is picking it up directly. Near field Cut 5 makes the noise follow the device
These readings narrow the field. They do not name a culprit. Each cut rules out half of what is left. Keep going until one path is all that remains.

Five cuts, most useful first

All five fit into an afternoon, and none of them needs equipment you do not already own.

Cut What you do What it tells you
1 Screw a 50 Ω dummy load onto the antenna socket and listen on the same frequency. Noise gone → it arrives through the antenna system (paths ① and ②). Noise still there → it comes in on the mains or from nearby equipment (③ and ④). This one cut halves the search.
2 Disconnect the antenna wire at the transformer. Leave the coax connected and running back to the shack. Noise still there → the braid is receiving it, and a choke has something to bite on (②). Noise collapses → the antenna wire is doing the listening and no choke will help (①).
3 Clip a ferrite bead on the coax and take it off again, several times. Level steps with the bead → you have found a common-mode path and a permanent choke is worth building. Level moves but does not come back, or only moves on some bands → you are moving a resonance, not removing a current. Section 03 explains why.
4 Run the radio from a battery, and unplug every charger, wall adapter and network cable in the room. Noise changes → it is coming in on the mains (③), and the fix belongs on the power lead, not on the coax.
5 Walk around with a portable shortwave receiver and hold it close to things that might be guilty. Noise jumps near one device → you have found it, and it is path ④. Noise is the same everywhere → go back to ①, ② or ③.
Two rules. Without them nothing else on this page means anything.

1. Compare, never measure. An S-meter reading is not a noise power measurement. It changes with AGC, with the preamp and attenuator, and — the part people forget — with whatever impedance the receiver is looking at. Fitting a dummy load changes that impedance, so part of what you see in cut 1 is not a change in noise at all.

2. Same hour, same frequency, same settings. The noise around you moves over minutes and hours as appliances and neighbours come and go. An A/B split across two evenings proves nothing.

Our own station, measured

You will read everywhere that an EFHW station needs a separate receiving loop. On this station it does not, and the numbers say so. They come from a year of PSK Reporter records analysed for BG1SB (grid ON80da), and they describe this site only.

Basis: efhw-knowledge/measurements/bg1sb_20m_efhw_case_study.md sections 2.5 and 2.6. These are relative values from PSK Reporter — not absolute noise power.

Measure your own noise floor before you accept somebody else's answer.
02 · Six sources

"Environmental interference" is six different problems

Each card below follows the same four headings, so you can read the choke verdict straight down the column and see where it differs.

1 · Power lines C

Where it comes from. Arcing across the utility's own hardware. A gap opens between two conductors — usually because fittings are loose, broken or spaced too closely — current crosses it, the air ionises, and broadband energy radiates away.

How to recognise it. On a 50 Hz grid it fires 100 times a second, because every cycle has two voltage peaks and two zero-crossings. On 60 Hz it is 120 Hz. On a scope you see two bands of noise drifting slowly upward, one from each half of the cycle. Two tests need no equipment at all: it goes quiet in the rain, because water bridges the gap, and it varies in the wind, because loose hardware moves.

A common mistake worth correcting. Corona discharge is almost never the cause. The ARRL is explicit: corona sits at low frequencies, it is a low-current effect, and it does not travel far. Blame corona and you will spend the day looking at the wrong thing, on the wrong pole.

Does a choke help? No. The energy runs along the power wiring and reaches you by radiating into the antenna wire — path ①. A coax choke works on the braid's common-mode path, and the noise is not on it.

What to do. Complain, with evidence. This is the one category where somebody else has to do the work, and in many places they are legally obliged to.

Sources: ARRL, Power Line Noise · IEEE Std 1897-2024

2 · Switch-mode power supplies C 算式

Where it comes from. A switching supply chops DC tens or hundreds of thousands of times a second. The harmonics of that chopping, plus the ringing on every edge, run right up through HF. The noise leaves through two routes: differential, between live and neutral, which an X capacitor suppresses; and common mode, between the conductors and earth, which needs a Y capacitor and a common-mode choke. Cheap supplies save money by leaving out those last two parts — the only two that do anything at HF.

How to recognise it. A comb of harmonics spaced at the switching frequency, drifting around as the load changes. On light load you often hear a slow wobble as the supply enters and leaves burst mode.

What "compliant" actually means. Household equipment has to meet the Class B conducted-emission limits. Here they are, with the voltage limit converted to the power it represents across 50 Ω:

Compare the top three rows with the bottom two. A device that scrapes through with no margin puts about the same level on its mains port as a quiet band puts at your receiver. Even after the house wiring re-radiates that at a small fraction of its conducted level, it can still be far above a quiet band. Passing the standard is not the same as not interfering. The limit is a deal between manufacturers and spectrum managers, not a promise that your HF reception will work.

Does a choke help? Not on the coax. Noise arriving on the mains never touches the coax, so a choke there does nothing at all.

What to do. Move the same component to the right conductor: a common-mode choke on the mains lead or the DC lead. Then check the USB and network cables, which are the ones most often overlooked.

Sources: GB/T 9254.1-2021 (≈ CISPR 32) · standard EMC practice for X and Y capacitors and common-mode chokes

3 · Electric vehicle chargers C

Where it comes from. Slow charging runs the mains through a charger inside the car: a switching converter of 3.3 to 11 kW. Fast charging does the conversion inside the charging post, at higher power and increasingly with silicon-carbide devices that switch even faster. Either way, the charging cable is several metres of multi-conductor cable running from a building to a vehicle — and its protective earth conductor is exactly as long as the antenna it forms. A kilowatt-class switching converter driving a long unshielded conductor, with no RF ground at either end.

How to recognise it. Broadband, strong while a car is charging, gone when the plug comes out. That in-and-out relationship is the useful part: it dates the interference and identifies the source even when you cannot see the charger.

Nobody has published a shortwave measurement of one of these. Every case report we could reach is about conducted- emission test failures, or about the 125 kHz band where charging posts interfere with keyless entry. We could not find a single measurement of a charger between 1.8 and 30 MHz. So what you have just read is mechanism, not measurement. We are not going to print a number we cannot show you, and you should be suspicious of anyone who does.

Does a choke help? Partly. Most of it arrives through path ①, radiating from the charging cable into your antenna wire. A coax choke only reaches the small share that arrives on the shield.

What to do. Talk to somebody, and change your own geometry. Establish the charging-times correlation first — that is the evidence that makes a conversation with the owner or the building manager possible. Then move the antenna, or accept those hours as lost.

Sources: GB/T 18487.1-2015 · published EV-charger EMC remediation case reports · the 125 kHz keyless-entry reports

4 · Solar inverters C

Where it comes from. Most modern string inverters have no transformer, so the panels and the grid share a common-mode path with nothing in between. Add the long DC run down from the roof, which forms a big loop, and switching devices with fast edges, and you have a strong common-mode source bolted to the same building as your antenna.

How to recognise it. It follows the sun and the inverter's output. Strongest around midday, gone at night, and it changes when a cloud passes.

Does a choke help? Partly. The paths are mixed. The part radiated into your antenna is out of reach. The part conducted into the building wiring can be dealt with, but with a choke on the mains, not on the coax.

What to do. Almost all of it is source-side work, which means not yours: LCL filters, common-mode reactors, active filtering and gate-drive tuning all live inside the inverter. What is yours is the same on/off evidence, and the same conversation.

Sources: ARRL RFI programme, which tracks solar alongside power lines and lighting

5 · Power-line communication C

Where it comes from. Broadband over Power Line is defined as carrying data over power wiring between 2 and 30 MHz. That is not near the amateur HF bands. It is the amateur HF bands, plus everything in between.

How to recognise it. This is the only entry here that is not a by-product. It is a modulated signal transmitted on purpose, inside your band, and it sounds like one.

Does a choke help? No, and it never will. The signal arrives in band, on the antenna, exactly where a wanted signal would arrive. There is no common-mode path to interrupt.

What to do. Frequency coordination, or a complaint. This is a regulatory problem, and treating it as an antenna problem just costs money.

Sources: the BPL band definition (2–30 MHz) · the national radio-interference complaint procedure

6 · LED lighting and grow lights C

Where it comes from. Every LED lamp contains a driver, and that driver is a small switching supply built to the tightest possible budget. The same cost pressure as card 2, at a smaller scale and with less room for filtering. High-power ballasts for grow lights are the industrial version of the same thing; the ARRL lists them as a category it gets a steady stream of questions about.

How to recognise it. It follows a switch. Noise appears when the lamp is on and disappears when it is off, which makes cut 5 decisive and makes this the easiest source on the list to identify.

Does a choke help? Partly. It depends entirely on which path the noise used, which is why the checking comes before the buying.

What to do. Replace the lamp with one that behaves. Cheaper and more reliable than filtering the consequences.

Sources: ARRL RFI programme (lighting devices category)

Eight rows. Only the last one is a job a coax choke can do properly.

One sentence produces this whole table. A choke only acts on the common-mode path in the conductor you put it on. That has three consequences, and they account for every "no" above:

1. It cannot touch noise the antenna wire picks up, because that noise is not on the coax.
2. It cannot touch noise arriving on the mains, because that noise never reaches the coax either.
3. It cannot touch noise that has already got onto the inner conductor, because by then the noise has left the path the choke can reach.
03 · Path ②: the coax shield

A choke does not care about frequency. It cares about which conductor.

Get this part right and everything else follows: where to fit the choke, why one sometimes makes things worse, and how many turns to wind.

Two things travel on a coax

One is your signal: a voltage difference between the inner conductor and the inside of the shield. The receiver sees that and nothing else. The other is current on the outside of the shield — your feedline acting as an antenna, picking up whatever is around it.

That second current cannot walk into the receiver on its own. Something has to turn it into a voltage difference first, and the turning happens wherever your system is unbalanced: the 49:1 transformer, the feedpoint, the chassis ground. Your feedline is an antenna wired into a converter you cannot remove.

A common-mode choke does one thing. It puts a high impedance in the path along the outside of the shield, so less current flows there. Your signal runs on the other side of the shield and is unaffected. The choke does not select a frequency, it selects a conductor.

That is also why it does nothing else. It will not improve your match, raise efficiency, or change SWR, because none of those depend on the common-mode path. If your SWR moved after you fitted a choke, something else moved — and it is worth finding out what.

How much noise it actually removes

Put an impedance in the common-mode loop and less current flows. To a first approximation:

A ≈ 20 · log₁₀( 1 + Z_cm / Z_loop )

Z_loop is not 50 Ω. It is the impedance of the loop the noise came from: the house wiring, the neighbour's appliance, the building's earth path. It changes a lot with frequency and you will usually not know it. So treat the table below as a division sum, not a measurement. Every cell is the formula above, and you can check any of them yourself.

Before you buy anything, look at the left column. A clip-on bead sits in the tens of ohms across most of HF. That is the top row: a few dB at best. To make a real difference a choke needs to reach kilohms, and that means a proper core with a proper number of turns through it.

Where that sum stops being true

The division sum is a rough picture. It gets three things wrong, and all three matter in the field.

1. A choke's impedance is not a plain resistance. It is a resistance plus a reactance, and the resistance is the useful part. A purely reactive choke absorbs nothing. It stores energy and hands it back somewhere else, which is how you end up with a resonance instead of attenuation.

2. Fitting a choke changes where the current goes. The sum pretends you are just adding a resistance to a fixed loop. In reality the coax is a significant fraction of a wavelength, the current peaks move, and a single choke often moves a resonance rather than removing a current. The classic symptom is "much better on 7 MHz and now worse on 5 MHz". If that happens, you moved the problem.

3. So fit two, not one. One at the feedpoint, one where the coax comes into the shack. That cuts the line into sections, and if each section is shorter than a quarter wave none of them can resonate. The two are not alternatives — they do different jobs.

A choke is not equally effective on every band

More turns give more impedance — it goes up with the square of the turn count. But the ferrite's permeability also changes with frequency. Measured values for the two mixes you are most likely to use:

So "one choke for 1.8–30 MHz" does not exist. Look at Mix 43: from about 7 MHz upward, μ″ — the loss term — catches up with and passes μ′. In a transformer that is bad news. In a choke it is good news, because a lossy choke absorbs the energy instead of storing and returning it, which is exactly what point 1 above asked for. Same material, different job, opposite verdict.

Receiving gives you one free advantage

Received signals are microvolts. A ferrite on a receive path never saturates, so none of the power-handling and heating rules that govern transmitting chokes apply here.

That cuts both ways. If a choke serves receive only — a separate receiving antenna, a T/R-split front end — you can wind on as many turns as you like and use a high-permeability mix, reaching an impedance no transmit path would survive. If the choke also carries your transmitter, it is limited by saturation and heat.

The best choke for receive may be one that would burn up on transmit. Before you copy somebody's recipe, ask which side of the T/R switch their numbers came from.

You need a counterpoise before you need a choke

An end-fed antenna needs a return path. With no return path, fitting a choke just makes the choke part of the antenna's load, and the current finds a third route out — through the chassis, down the mains, into everything you were trying to protect. A choke does not replace a counterpoise. The counterpoise is what lets the choke work.
And do not choke at the feedpoint if the coax is your counterpoise. Do that and you cut the antenna's own return path: SWR shifts and efficiency drops. In that arrangement the choke has to sit away from the feedpoint — the ARRL Handbook figure is at least 10 feet, about 3 m. If you have a separate counterpoise wire, the opposite is true: the feedpoint is the best place to choke, because the current you are blocking is exactly the current the antenna wanted to send down the braid.

04 · The other three paths

When a choke is the wrong tool

Three of the four paths are out of a coax choke's reach. Each has its own giveaway, and none of the fixes is a ferrite on the feedline.

Path ③: mains and earth loops

The noise source shares a supply with your rig. Its rubbish travels down the wiring, through the power supply, and onto the chassis — and the chassis is what the receiver measures against. None of this route involves the coax, which is why cut 1 does not clear it.

How to recognise it. Cut 1 leaves it standing, and cut 4 changes it. Those two results together are the diagnosis.

What to do. Take the choke you were about to put on the coax and put it on the conductor that is actually carrying the noise: the mains lead or the DC lead. Check USB and network cables too.
Never disconnect the mains safety earth to quieten a noise problem. A common-mode choke passes mains frequency and DC while blocking RF, which is exactly what you want: the safety earth stays connected and the noise is still stopped. Disconnecting it removes the protection that keeps you alive, and in our experience it does not even fix the noise reliably.

Path ①: antenna position

An end-fed half wave is fed at the point of highest voltage on the wire. A high-voltage point is naturally sensitive to nearby electric fields, which is how lamp drivers and small switching supplies mostly couple. The shape that makes an EFHW convenient to hang is also the shape that makes it a good listener for everything in the building.

How to recognise it. A choke changes nothing, but moving the antenna changes the noise a lot. Cut 2 clears the noise, telling you the wire is doing the listening rather than the braid.

What to do. Position, and only position: higher, further from the building, a different direction, or a separate receiving antenna. No amount of ferrite fixes this, and no purchase will either.

Path ④: near field

Your rig, your SDR, a USB lead, a network cable: any of these is a small antenna sitting on your desk, listening to whatever is switching next to it. It is the least exotic path and the easiest to overlook, because the culprit is usually something you installed yourself and therefore trust.

How to recognise it. Cut 5 makes the noise follow the device as you walk around the house.

What to do. Distance first, ferrites second. Moving a switch-mode adapter two metres away from the rig usually beats anything you can do to the antenna system.
05 · Prove it worked

How to know, without fooling yourself

The two rules from section 01 are still in force. Everything here is about making them easy to follow.

Compare, never measure. And change one thing at a time. Same hour, same frequency, same AGC, same preamp, same attenuator. Put everything back the way it was before you try the next thing.

Equipment you may already have

You do not need a laboratory. If your station runs through the MRRC stack you already have an S-meter feed and a DSP spectrum display, and both are enough to A/B a choke as long as you freeze the receiver settings. The spectrum display is the better of the two, because it shows the shape of the noise as well as its level — and a choke that moves a resonance looks completely different from one that removes a current.

For a number you can compare across days, the PSK Reporter analysis this station uses is open to anyone with a callsign and some patience. It works out a noise floor from which stations you can hear, and the method is written up on the measured antenna profile page. Run it before the change and again after, then compare.

What you cannot claim. One daytime A/B cannot pin down how much you improved, because the noise around you changes between the two halves of the test. What you can claim is narrower and still worth having: with settings frozen, at these frequencies and this time of day, the noise was repeatably lower with the choke fitted. Anything stronger than that needs a longer baseline than most of us will collect — and be careful of anyone who produces a confident figure from one afternoon.
06 · What a choke cannot fix

The same rule, seen from the other side

Section 02 gave you a table. This is the same table read as a list of things not to waste a ferrite on.

  • Noise the antenna wire picks up. Not on the coax, so out of reach.
  • Noise arriving on the mains. Same reason, different conductor.
  • Noise already on the inner conductor. By then it has left the path a choke can reach.
  • In-band signals such as PLC or BPL. There is no common-mode path to interrupt. It is a signal in your band, and it needs a regulator rather than a core.

When a second antenna is the honest answer

If your station really is buried in local noise, the standard answer is to split transmit from receive: keep the EFHW for transmitting, add an active magnetic loop for receiving, point its null at the noise, and switch between them with a T/R relay or the rig's own receive antenna socket. It works. The products exist.

But only if you actually have the problem. Section 01 shows a station that measured its own noise floor, found it clean, and concluded from the data that a receiving loop would have been money spent on a disease it did not have. Do that measurement before you buy anything. It costs less than the loop, and it might save you the whole purchase.

When the fix belongs to somebody else

Power-line arcing and a neighbour's charger are the two cases you cannot fix at the source yourself. Both need evidence and a channel. In China that channel is the 12381 hotline, opened on 20 March 2023 as the radio-interference complaint centre under the MIIT public-service platform.

One detail decides whether your complaint goes anywhere: shortwave and satellite complaints are not handled by local radio offices. The local office forwards them to the National Radio Monitoring Center and the ministry's radio bureau. An HF complaint travels upward, not sideways.

Build the log before you call: when it started, which bands, which modes, what it sounds like, and a 30 to 60 second recording in both AM and SSB. This is the same list the ARRL recommends to its own members, and it is what turns "my radio is noisy" into a case.

The one rule with no exceptions. Never disconnect the mains safety earth, and never suggest it to anyone else. Every fix on this page works with the safety earth connected, because a choke passes mains frequency and DC while blocking RF. Anything that needs the safety earth out of the way is not a fix. It is a hazard.
