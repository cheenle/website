Where received noise
actually gets in

Every dB of received noise enters through one of four doors. They have different causes, different signatures and different cures, and a common-mode choke only reaches one of them. This page is about finding out which door your noise uses before you spend money, because on a quiet station the honest answer is that you should buy nothing at all. Every claim carries a C for a checkable source or an 算式 for arithmetic you can redo on paper.

01 · Measure first, buy later

Four doors, and the cuts that separate them

Received noise enters an EFHW station through four paths. The ARRL’s own guidance for interference hunting is to ask “where is this coming from” before asking “what is producing it”, and the same discipline pays here: you cannot pick the right cure until you know the path.

Door Path Coupling How it shows up
① Element The antenna wire picks the noise up directly. Near-field electric, and far field Cut 2 barely changes it
② Shield Common-mode current on the outside of the coax braid, running through the noise field all the way from the feedpoint to the shack. Conducted along the braid, plus field coupling Cut 2 collapses it; a clip-on bead changes it
③ Mains Noise conducted in on the supply wiring, through the PSU, into the receiver’s ground reference. Conducted Cut 4 changes it
④ Near field The rig, the SDR, a USB lead or an Ethernet run is itself the receiving antenna. Near-field Cut 5 makes the noise follow the device
Read the last column as a pointer, not a proof. Each cut narrows the candidates; none of them identifies a culprit on its own. You are eliminating doors, one at a time, until one is left standing.

Five cuts, in the order they pay

All five fit in one afternoon and need nothing you do not already own. Cut 1 carries the most information per minute, so start there and stop as soon as a door is eliminated.

Cut What you do What the reading means
1 Screw a 50 Ω dummy load onto the antenna port and listen on the same frequency. Noise disappears → it arrives via the antenna system (door ① or ②). Noise stays → it comes in on the mains or from the near field (door ③ or ④). This one cut halves the search space.
2 Disconnect the antenna wire at the transformer but leave the coax connected and running to the shack. Noise stays → the braid is receiving it, and a choke has a target (door ②). Noise collapses → the antenna wire itself is doing the listening, and no choke will help (door ①).
3 Clip a ferrite bead onto the coax and A/B it in and out, several times. Level steps with the bead → you have confirmed a common-mode path, and a permanent choke is worth building. Level moves but not back, or moves only at some frequencies → you are shifting a resonance, not removing a current (see the divider section).
4 Run the radio from a battery and unplug every charger, every wall-wart and every Ethernet run in the room. Noise changes → door ③, and the cure belongs on the mains lead, not on the coax.
5 Walk the house with a portable shortwave receiver, holding it close to suspect devices. Noise rises sharply near one device → door ④, and you have found the culprit. Noise that is uniform everywhere points back at doors ①, ② or ③.
Two rules, or nothing below this line is trustworthy.
1. Relative readings only. An S-meter reading is not a noise power measurement. It moves with AGC, with preamp and attenuator settings, and — the part people miss — with the source impedance the receiver is looking at. Swapping in a dummy load changes that impedance, so part of the level shift in cut 1 is not a noise change at all. Compare, never measure absolutely.
2. Same hour, same frequency, same settings. The ambient noise field moves over minutes and hours as appliances and neighbours come and go. An A/B split across two evenings proves nothing.

A worked counter-example from this station

The advice to add a receiving loop is repeated everywhere as if every EFHW station needs one. On this station the opposite is true, and it is worth showing how a measurement settles the question rather than a reputation. Values below are from a full year of PSK Reporter records analysed for BG1SB (grid ON80da), and they describe this site only.

Basis: efhw-knowledge/measurements/bg1sb_20m_efhw_case_study.md §2.5 and §2.6 (2026-06-14). Relative values from PSK Reporter, not absolute noise power.
Quantity Measured value Reading
Weakest signal decoded, quietest direction −23 dB SNR The FT8 decoder’s limit is about −24 dB, so the station is within a dB of theoretical sensitivity
Noise floor by direction Uniform, no directional source No direction is being swallowed by a local noise source
DX share, receive vs transmit 29.9% RX against 20.6% TX Receive reaches further than transmit, so receive is not the bottleneck
So the conclusion is “buy nothing”, and it is a measured conclusion. The mechanism behind the popular advice is sound — a station buried in local noise really is deaf on receive, and a loop with a sharp null really can help. But the premise does not hold here, so the conclusion does not either. Measure your own noise floor before you accept somebody else’s cure.
